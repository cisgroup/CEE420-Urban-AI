Lecture 08 turned a graph into something a model can learn on. The adjacency matrix sums a node's neighbours, a normalisation turns the sum into an average, a learned linear map after the average makes a graph convolutional layer, and two layers trained on a few labelled nodes name the rest. The lecture then asked the one question that matters for your project, whether the graph helps, and answered it against a model with the same weights that never sees the graph: on the speed limits OpenStreetMap does not know in Princeton, and on the traffic of 207 sensors in Los Angeles. These four notebooks put all of it on your laptop. You run and read. You do not write code. None of it is assessed.

Start with =08_01=. Its first cell prints the seed, the counts and whether PyTorch and PyTorch Geometric are ready; if that works your environment is ready for the rest. Then read in order. A number that follows from arithmetic alone, the toy's and the traffic baselines', equals the lecture's to the last digit. A number that comes out of training is printed beside the lecture's, and the reference numbers the course pipeline computed from the same files travel in =data/ground_truth.json=.

Every computation is one call into =graph_tools.py=, the kit's toolbox, which the lecture's runs and figures import too. It holds the adjacency matrix and its normalisations, message passing, the Laplacian, the graph convolutional layer in NumPy and in PyTorch, the model without a graph, the training loop on the labelled nodes, the street experiment with its splits and repeated draws, the bridge to PyTorch Geometric, the sensor graph, the forecasting baselines and the plots, written with a sentence of explanation on each. The matrices are dense, so every one of them can be printed and checked by hand, and you can open the file and read how any step works.

Training runs on one processor thread, which the toolbox sets, so a run gives the same numbers every time on one machine. Another machine may keep the weights of a different epoch and print an accuracy a point away from the lecture's. That is expected, and the notebooks print the lecture's number beside yours. The cells that need PyTorch or PyTorch Geometric skip cleanly with one line when the library is missing.

Each notebook ends with "Your turn": a cell that runs as it is, with one marked value to change. Change it, run again, and answer one question in words. The answers come in the Friday post, not in the kit. "Check your understanding" asks four questions you can answer from what the notebook showed.

The data in =data/= is the lecture's running example cut to what a laptop needs. The street segments are Lecture 07's drive network of Princeton from OpenStreetMap read with one node per segment: the segment's length and bearing, its road class, the speed limit of its =maxspeed= tag where the map has one, the 64 numbers of the 2021 satellite embedding at its midpoint, the street graph and the proximity graph, and the lines for the maps. The lecture's own runs travel with them, its one run in full and its twenty draws of every model, so a notebook can show the table without training for an hour. The traffic data is METR-LA as Li and colleagues released it in 2018: the speeds of 207 loop detectors every five minutes from 1 March to 27 June 2012, the road distances between the sensors and their positions. =data/scene.json= records the rules of every run, the lecture's numbers, the choices the pipeline made and the provenance of every file.
